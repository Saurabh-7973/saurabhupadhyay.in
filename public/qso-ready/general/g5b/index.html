G5B: The decibel; current and voltage dividers; electrical power calculations; sine wave root-mean-square (RMS) values; PEP calculations

Group G5B of the General pool (2023–2027) has 14 active questions. One of them is on every General exam. Each is shown word for word; open it to see the correct answer and our explanation.

G5B01

What dB change represents a factor of two increase or decrease in power?

  1. A Approximately 2 dB
  2. B Approximately 3 dB
  3. C Approximately 6 dB
  4. D Approximately 9 dB
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Approximately 3 dB

Why B is right. B. Doubling or halving power is a change of about 3 dB.

Decibels compare two powers on a logarithmic scale. To convert a power ratio to dB, take 10 times the base-10 logarithm of the ratio. For a ratio of 2, that comes to about 3 dB. Halving power is about minus 3 dB.

dB = 10 × log10(P2 / P1)
dB = 10 × log10(2)
dB = 10 × 0.301 = 3.01 dB
= Approximately 3 dB

Why not C? 6 dB is about four times the power, not two.

G5B02

How does the total current relate to the individual currents in a circuit of parallel resistors?

  1. A It equals the average of the branch currents
  2. B It decreases as more parallel branches are added to the circuit
  3. C It equals the sum of the currents through each branch
  4. D It is the sum of the reciprocal of each individual voltage drop
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. It equals the sum of the currents through each branch

Why C is right. C. Every parallel branch draws its own current from the same source, so the source must supply all of them added together.

Each branch sits across the same voltage. Adding more branches adds more paths, and the total current goes up.

Why not B? Each new branch adds current, so the total rises.

G5B03

How many watts of electrical power are consumed if 400 VDC is supplied to an 800-ohm load?

  1. A 0.5 watts
  2. B 200 watts
  3. C 400 watts
  4. D 3200 watts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 200 watts

Why B is right. B. 400 squared divided by 800 gives 200 watts.

When you know voltage and resistance, power is voltage squared divided by resistance. Here E is in volts, R is in ohms and P comes out in watts.

P = E² / R
P = 400² / 800
P = 160,000 / 800 = 200 W
= 200 watts

Why not A? 0.5 is 400 divided by 800, which gives the current in amperes, not the power.

G5B04

How many watts of electrical power are consumed by a 12 VDC light bulb that draws 0.2 amperes?

  1. A 2.4 watts
  2. B 24 watts
  3. C 6 watts
  4. D 60 watts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 2.4 watts

Why A is right. A. 12 volts times 0.2 amperes is 2.4 watts.

Power is voltage times current. With volts and amperes, the answer is in watts.

P = E × I
P = 12 V × 0.2 A
P = 2.4 W
= 2.4 watts

Why not B? 24 watts comes from a decimal slip; 12 × 0.2 is 2.4.

G5B05

How many watts are consumed when a current of 7.0 milliamperes flows through a 1,250-ohm resistance?

  1. A Approximately 61 milliwatts
  2. B Approximately 61 watts
  3. C Approximately 11 milliwatts
  4. D Approximately 11 watts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Approximately 61 milliwatts

Why A is right. A. The power is about 61 milliwatts.

When you know current and resistance, power is current squared times resistance. First change milliamperes to amperes: 7.0 mA is 0.0070 A. A milliwatt is one thousandth of a watt.

P = I² × R
I = 7.0 mA = 0.0070 A
P = 0.0070² × 1250 = 0.000049 × 1250
P = 0.06125 W = 61.25 mW
= 61.25 milliwatts

Why not B? 61 watts forgets to convert milliamperes to amperes first.

G5B06

What is the PEP produced by 200 volts peak-to-peak across a 50-ohm dummy load?

  1. A 1.4 watts
  2. B 100 watts
  3. C 353.5 watts
  4. D 400 watts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 100 watts

Why B is right. B. 200 volts peak-to-peak across 50 ohms gives a PEP of 100 watts.

PEP, peak envelope power, is the average power during one RF cycle at the highest point of the signal. Peak voltage is half the peak-to-peak value. For a sine wave, RMS voltage, the heating-equivalent value, is peak divided by the square root of 2. Then power is RMS voltage squared over resistance.

PEP = (Vpeak / √2)² / R
Vpeak = 200 V / 2 = 100 V
Vrms = 100 V / √2 = 70.71 V
PEP = 70.71² / 50 = 5000 / 50 = 100 W
= 100 watts

Why not D? 400 watts comes from treating the 200 volts peak-to-peak as if it were the peak voltage.

G5B07

What value of an AC signal produces the same power dissipation in a resistor as a DC voltage of the same value?

  1. A The peak-to-peak value
  2. B The peak value
  3. C The RMS value
  4. D The reciprocal of the RMS value
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. The RMS value

Why C is right. C. The RMS value of AC heats a resistor the same as an equal DC voltage.

RMS stands for root mean square. It is a way of averaging an AC wave that matches its heating effect. So 120 volts RMS AC warms a resistor exactly as much as 120 volts DC.

Why not B? A sine wave reaches its peak only for an instant, so the peak value overstates the heating.

G5B08

What is the peak-to-peak voltage of a sine wave with an RMS voltage of 120 volts?

  1. A 84.8 volts
  2. B 169.7 volts
  3. C 240.0 volts
  4. D 339.4 volts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 339.4 volts

Why D is right. D. 120 volts RMS is 339.4 volts peak-to-peak.

For a sine wave, peak voltage is RMS voltage times the square root of 2, about 1.4142. Peak-to-peak runs from the top of the wave to the bottom, which is twice the peak.

Vpp = 2 × √2 × Vrms
Vpeak = 120 V × 1.4142 = 169.7 V
Vpp = 2 × 169.7 V = 339.4 V
= 339.4 volts

Why not B? 169.7 volts is the peak value, only half the peak-to-peak.

G5B09

What is the RMS voltage of a sine wave with a value of 17 volts peak?

  1. A 8.5 volts
  2. B 12 volts
  3. C 24 volts
  4. D 34 volts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 12 volts

Why B is right. B. 17 volts peak is about 12 volts RMS.

For a sine wave, RMS voltage is the peak voltage divided by the square root of 2, about 1.4142. RMS is the value that gives the same heating as DC.

Vrms = Vpeak / √2
Vrms = 17 V / 1.4142
Vrms = 12.02 V, about 12 V
= 12 volts

Why not D? 34 volts is twice the peak, which is the peak-to-peak value.

G5B10

What percentage of power loss is equivalent to a loss of 1 dB?

  1. A 10.9 percent
  2. B 12.2 percent
  3. C 20.6 percent
  4. D 25.9 percent
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 20.6 percent

Why C is right. C. A 1 dB loss leaves about 79.4 percent of the power, so 20.6 percent is lost.

A loss of 1 dB means the remaining power is 10 raised to minus 1 tenth times the original. Subtract that fraction from 1 to find the part that was lost.

loss = (1 - 10^(-dB / 10)) × 100%
remaining = 10^(-1 / 10) = 0.7943
loss = (1 - 0.7943) × 100% = 20.6%
= 20.6 percent

Why not D? 25.9 percent is the loss you would get from about 1.3 dB, not 1 dB.

G5B11

What is the ratio of PEP to average power for an unmodulated carrier?

  1. A 0.707
  2. B 1.00
  3. C 1.414
  4. D 2.00
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 1.00

Why B is right. B. For an unmodulated carrier, PEP and average power are equal, a ratio of 1.00.

PEP is the average power during one RF cycle at the peak of the signal's envelope. The envelope is the outline of the signal's peaks. An unmodulated carrier never changes in strength, so every cycle is the same as the peak cycle. Its PEP equals its average power.

ratio = PEP / average power
steady carrier: every RF cycle has the same peak
PEP = average power
ratio = 1.00
= 1.00

Why not C? 1.414 is the ratio of a sine wave's peak voltage to its RMS voltage, not a power ratio.

G5B12

What is the RMS voltage across a 50-ohm dummy load dissipating 1200 watts?

  1. A 173 volts
  2. B 245 volts
  3. C 346 volts
  4. D 692 volts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 245 volts

Why B is right. B. A 50-ohm load taking 1200 watts has about 245 volts RMS across it.

Power equals voltage squared divided by resistance. Turn that around and voltage is the square root of power times resistance. Using RMS power gives RMS voltage.

E = √(P × R)
E = √(1200 × 50)
E = √60,000 = 244.9 V, about 245 V
= 245 volts

Why not C? 346 volts is about the peak voltage, which is the RMS value times 1.414.

G5B13

What is the output PEP of an unmodulated carrier if the average power is 1060 watts?

  1. A 530 watts
  2. B 1060 watts
  3. C 1500 watts
  4. D 2120 watts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 1060 watts

Why B is right. B. An unmodulated carrier's PEP equals its average power, 1060 watts.

A carrier with no modulation stays at one steady strength. Its peak cycle is the same as every other cycle, so the PEP equals the average power. The ratio is 1.00.

PEP = average power × 1.00
PEP = 1060 W × 1.00
PEP = 1060 W
= 1060 watts

Why not D? 2120 watts would double the power, but a steady carrier has no peaks above its average.

G5B14

What is the output PEP of 500 volts peak-to-peak across a 50-ohm load?

  1. A 8.75 watts
  2. B 625 watts
  3. C 2500 watts
  4. D 5000 watts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 625 watts

Why B is right. B. 500 volts peak-to-peak across 50 ohms gives a PEP of 625 watts.

Peak voltage is half the peak-to-peak value. RMS voltage of a sine wave is peak divided by the square root of 2. PEP is then RMS voltage squared divided by the load resistance.

PEP = (Vpeak / √2)² / R
Vpeak = 500 V / 2 = 250 V
Vrms = 250 V / √2 = 176.8 V
PEP = 176.8² / 50 = 31,250 / 50 = 625 W
= 625 watts

Why not C? 2500 watts comes from treating the 500 volts peak-to-peak as if it were the peak voltage.
