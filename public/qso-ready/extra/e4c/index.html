E4C: Receiver performance: phase noise, noise floor, image rejection, minimum detectable signal (MDS), increasing signal-to-noise ratio and dynamic range, noise figure, reciprocal mixing; selectivity; SDR non-linearity; use of attenuators at low frequencies

Group E4C of the Amateur Extra pool (2024–2028) has 14 active questions. One of them is on every Amateur Extra exam. Each is shown word for word; open it to see the correct answer and our explanation.

E4C01

What is an effect of excessive phase noise in an SDR receiver’s master clock oscillator?

  1. A It limits the receiver’s ability to receive strong signals
  2. B It can affect the receiver’s frequency calibration
  3. C It decreases the receiver’s third-order intercept point
  4. D It can combine with strong signals on nearby frequencies to generate interference
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. It can combine with strong signals on nearby frequencies to generate interference

Why D is right. D. A noisy clock spreads its noise onto strong nearby signals during sampling, and some of that noise lands on the frequency you are receiving.

Phase noise is small, random jitter in an oscillator's timing. It shows up as a skirt of noise on both sides of the oscillator frequency. In an SDR the master clock sets the sampling instants, so a strong signal close to your frequency gets smeared by that jitter, raising the noise you hear. This is called reciprocal mixing.

Why not B? Calibration depends on the clock's average frequency, not its short-term jitter.

E4C02

Which of the following receiver circuits can be effective in eliminating interference from strong out-of-band signals?

  1. A A front-end filter or preselector
  2. B A narrow IF filter
  3. C A notch filter
  4. D A properly adjusted product detector
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. A front-end filter or preselector

Why A is right. A. A front-end filter weakens strong signals outside the band before they reach the first amplifier and mixer, where they would cause overload.

Out-of-band signals, such as a nearby broadcast station, can overload a receiver's first stages and create false signals. A preselector is a tuned filter at the input. It passes the band you want and weakens the rest, so later circuits only see signals they can handle.

Why not B? An IF filter comes after the mixer, so overload from the strong signal has already happened.

E4C03

What is the term for the suppression in an FM receiver of one signal by another stronger signal on the same frequency?

  1. A Desensitization
  2. B Cross-modulation interference
  3. C Capture effect
  4. D Frequency discrimination
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Capture effect

Why C is right. C. In FM, the stronger of two signals on one frequency takes over the receiver and the weaker one nearly vanishes; this is the capture effect.

An FM receiver's limiter and detector respond mostly to the strongest signal present. If one signal is a few dB stronger, it controls the output. That is why on a repeater you often hear only the stronger of two stations that transmit at once.

Why not A? Desensitization is lost sensitivity from a strong signal on a nearby frequency, not the same frequency.

E4C04

What is the noise figure of a receiver?

  1. A The ratio of atmospheric noise to phase noise
  2. B The ratio of the noise bandwidth in hertz to the theoretical bandwidth of a resistive network
  3. C The ratio in dB of the noise generated in the receiver to atmospheric noise
  4. D The ratio in dB of the noise generated by the receiver to the theoretical minimum noise
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. The ratio in dB of the noise generated by the receiver to the theoretical minimum noise

Why D is right. D. Noise figure tells, in dB, how much more noise a receiver adds than a perfect receiver would.

Every resistor and transistor produces some noise of its own. A perfect receiver would add none, so its noise figure would be 0 dB. A receiver with a 3 dB noise figure makes the signal-to-noise ratio 3 dB worse than that ideal. Lower is better, especially for weak-signal VHF and UHF work.

Why not C? Atmospheric noise comes from outside; noise figure counts only noise the receiver creates.

E4C05

What does a receiver noise floor of -174 dBm represent?

  1. A The receiver noise is 6 dB above the theoretical minimum
  2. B The theoretical noise in a 1 Hz bandwidth at the input of a perfect receiver at room temperature
  3. C The noise figure of a 1 Hz bandwidth receiver
  4. D The receiver noise is 3 dB above theoretical minimum
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. The theoretical noise in a 1 Hz bandwidth at the input of a perfect receiver at room temperature

Why B is right. B. -174 dBm is the thermal noise power in a 1 Hz bandwidth at room temperature, a floor even a perfect receiver cannot go below.

Thermal noise power is P = kTB: k is Boltzmann's constant (1.38 × 10^-23 joules per kelvin), T is temperature in kelvin, and B is bandwidth in hertz. At 290 kelvin and 1 Hz, P is about 4 × 10^-21 W, which is -174 dBm. A wider bandwidth or a noisier receiver raises the floor.

Why not C? Noise figure is a ratio in dB, not a power level in dBm.

E4C06

How much does increasing a receiver’s bandwidth from 50 Hz to 1,000 Hz increase the receiver’s noise floor?

  1. A 3 dB
  2. B 5 dB
  3. C 10 dB
  4. D 13 dB
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 13 dB

Why D is right. D. Noise power grows in step with bandwidth, and 20 times the bandwidth means about 13 dB more noise.

Thermal noise is spread evenly across frequency, so a receiver lets in noise in proportion to how wide its passband is. Going from 50 Hz to 1,000 Hz lets in 20 times as much noise power. Converting that ratio to decibels gives about 13 dB.

Increase (dB) = 10 × log10(B2 / B1)
Increase = 10 × log10(1,000 Hz / 50 Hz)
= 10 × log10(20)
= 10 × 1.301 = 13.0 dB
= 13 dB

Why not C? 10 dB would be a tenfold increase, from 50 Hz to 500 Hz.

E4C07

What does the MDS of a receiver represent?

  1. A The meter display sensitivity
  2. B The minimum discernible signal
  3. C The modulation distortion specification
  4. D The maximum detectable spectrum
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. The minimum discernible signal

Why B is right. B. MDS stands for minimum discernible signal, the weakest signal a receiver can detect above its own noise.

MDS is stated in dBm for a given bandwidth, such as -130 dBm in 500 Hz. It is often defined as the signal level equal to the receiver's noise, which raises the output 3 dB above noise alone. A lower (more negative) MDS means a more sensitive receiver.

Why not D? MDS is a single weak-signal level, not a span of frequencies.

E4C08

An SDR receiver is overloaded when input signals exceed what level?

  1. A One-half of the maximum sample rate
  2. B One-half of the maximum sampling buffer size
  3. C The maximum count value of the analog-to-digital converter
  4. D The reference voltage of the analog-to-digital converter
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. The reference voltage of the analog-to-digital converter

Why D is right. D. The converter can only report voltages up to its reference voltage; larger inputs are clipped, which distorts everything being digitized.

An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) compares the input with a reference voltage. Every input above that level gives the same top value, so the waveform's peaks are flattened. Clipping creates false signals across the band. That is why SDRs show an overload warning and offer attenuators.

Why not A? The sample rate limits the frequency range, not how strong a signal can be.

E4C09

Which of the following choices is a good reason for selecting a high IF for a superheterodyne HF or VHF communications receiver?

  1. A Fewer components in the receiver
  2. B Reduced drift
  3. C Easier for front-end circuitry to eliminate image responses
  4. D Improved receiver noise figure
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Easier for front-end circuitry to eliminate image responses

Why C is right. C. With a high IF, the image frequency lies far from the wanted signal, so the front-end filter can reject it easily.

A superheterodyne mixes the incoming signal with a local oscillator to make a fixed intermediate frequency (IF). A second, unwanted input frequency, the image, also mixes to that IF; it sits twice the IF away from the wanted signal. A high IF pushes the image far away, where the input filter weakens it.

Why not D? Noise figure depends mostly on the first amplifier and mixer, not on the IF chosen.

E4C10

What is an advantage of having a variety of receiver bandwidths from which to select?

  1. A The noise figure of the RF amplifier can be adjusted to match the modulation type, thus increasing receiver sensitivity
  2. B Receiver power consumption can be reduced when wider bandwidth is not required
  3. C Receive bandwidth can be set to match the modulation bandwidth, maximizing signal-to-noise ratio and minimizing interference
  4. D Multiple frequencies can be received simultaneously if desired
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Receive bandwidth can be set to match the modulation bandwidth, maximizing signal-to-noise ratio and minimizing interference

Why C is right. C. Matching the filter to the signal's width lets in all of the signal but as little noise and nearby interference as possible.

A CW signal needs a few hundred hertz, SSB voice about 2.4 kHz, and AM about 6 kHz. Extra bandwidth only adds noise and neighbors, while too little cuts off part of the signal. Choosing the right filter for each mode gives the best signal-to-noise ratio.

Why not A? Selecting a bandwidth does not change the RF amplifier's noise figure.

E4C11

Why does input attenuation reduce receiver overload on the lower frequency HF bands with little or no impact on signal-to-noise ratio?

  1. A The attenuator has a low-pass filter to increase the strength of lower frequency signals
  2. B The attenuator has a noise filter to suppress interference
  3. C Signals are attenuated separately from the noise
  4. D Atmospheric noise is generally greater than internally generated noise even after attenuation
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Atmospheric noise is generally greater than internally generated noise even after attenuation

Why D is right. D. On the low HF bands, outside atmospheric noise is far stronger than the receiver's own noise, so cutting signal and noise together keeps their ratio the same.

An attenuator lowers the signal and the outside noise arriving with it by the same amount. As long as that outside noise stays above the receiver's internal noise, the signal-to-noise ratio does not change. Meanwhile, strong signals drop to levels the receiver can handle without overload.

Why not C? An attenuator treats signal and noise alike; it cannot separate them.

E4C12

How does a narrow-band roofing filter affect receiver performance?

  1. A It improves sensitivity by reducing front-end noise
  2. B It improves intelligibility by using low Q circuitry to reduce ringing
  3. C It improves blocking dynamic range by attenuating strong signals near the receive frequency
  4. D All these choices are correct
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. It improves blocking dynamic range by attenuating strong signals near the receive frequency

Why C is right. C. A narrow roofing filter early in the receiver stops strong nearby signals before later stages, so those stages do not compress.

Blocking dynamic range is the span between the weakest signal you can hear and the strongest nearby signal that starts reducing the receiver's gain. A roofing filter, often a crystal filter a few kHz wide, sits right after the first mixer. Signals outside its passband never reach the amplifiers and DSP that follow.

Why not A? The filter does not lower the noise made by the front end itself.

E4C13

What is reciprocal mixing?

  1. A Two out-of-band signals mixing to generate an in-band spurious signal
  2. B In-phase signals cancelling in a mixer resulting in loss of receiver sensitivity
  3. C Two digital signals combining from alternate time slots
  4. D Local oscillator phase noise mixing with adjacent strong signals to create interference to desired signals
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Local oscillator phase noise mixing with adjacent strong signals to create interference to desired signals

Why D is right. D. Reciprocal mixing happens when the noise around a local oscillator mixes with a strong nearby signal and lands on the frequency you want.

A real local oscillator is not one pure frequency; it has phase noise, a faint skirt on either side. When a strong signal near your frequency mixes with that skirt, it produces noise inside your passband. You hear the hiss rise when the strong station transmits, even though it is outside your filter.

Why not A? Two signals mixing into a new in-band signal is intermodulation, not reciprocal mixing.

E4C14

What is the purpose of the receiver IF Shift control?

  1. A To permit listening on a different frequency from the transmitting frequency
  2. B To change frequency rapidly
  3. C To reduce interference from stations transmitting on adjacent frequencies
  4. D To tune in stations slightly off frequency without changing the transmit frequency
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. To reduce interference from stations transmitting on adjacent frequencies

Why C is right. C. IF shift slides the filter's passband up or down relative to the signal so a station on an adjacent frequency falls outside it.

The receive frequency stays the same, but the filter window moves. If an interfering signal sits slightly above your station, shift the passband down so the interference drops off the edge. You may lose a little high or low audio, but the signal is often easier to understand.

Why not D? Tuning an off-frequency station without moving your transmit frequency is RIT, receiver incremental tuning.
