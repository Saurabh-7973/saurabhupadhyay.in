T3C: Propagation modes: sporadic E, meteor scatter, auroral propagation, tropospheric ducting; F region skip; Line of sight and radio horizon

Group T3C of the Technician pool (2026–2030) has 11 active questions. One of them is on every Technician exam. Each is shown word for word; open it to see the correct answer and our explanation.

T3C01

Why are simplex UHF signals rarely heard beyond their radio horizon?

  1. A They are too weak to go very far
  2. B FCC regulations prohibit them from going more than 50 miles
  3. C UHF signals are usually not propagated by the ionosphere
  4. D UHF signals are absorbed by the ionospheric D region
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. UHF signals are usually not propagated by the ionosphere

Why C is right. C. The ionosphere usually does not bend UHF signals back to Earth.

Signals at UHF pass straight through the ionosphere and go out into space. Without that bounce, a simplex UHF signal mostly travels only as far as the radio horizon.

Why not B? No FCC rule limits how far a signal may travel.

T3C02

What is a characteristic of HF communication compared with communications on VHF and higher frequencies?

  1. A HF antennas are generally smaller
  2. B HF accommodates wider bandwidth signals
  3. C Long-distance ionospheric propagation is far more common on HF
  4. D There is less atmospheric interference (static) on HF
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Long-distance ionospheric propagation is far more common on HF

Why C is right. C. Long-distance ionospheric propagation is far more common on HF.

The ionosphere bends HF signals back to Earth often, allowing contacts across oceans. VHF and higher signals usually pass through it, so they stay closer to line of sight.

Why not A? HF waves are longer, so HF antennas are usually larger, not smaller.

T3C03

What is one characteristic of VHF signals received via auroral backscatter?

  1. A They are often received from 10,000 miles or more
  2. B They are distorted, with a characteristic raspy sound
  3. C They occur only during winter nighttime hours
  4. D They are generally strongest when your antenna is aimed west
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. They are distorted, with a characteristic raspy sound

Why B is right. B. Auroral signals sound distorted and raspy.

The aurora is a moving, uneven region of charged particles. Signals scattered off it get spread and smeared in frequency. The result is a rough, buzzing sound, which is why Morse code works better than voice.

Why not D? The aurora lies toward the poles, so stations in the US usually aim north, not west.

T3C04

Which of the following types of propagation is most commonly associated with occasional strong signals on the 10-, 6-, and 2-meter bands from beyond the radio horizon?

  1. A Backscatter
  2. B Sporadic E
  3. C D region absorption
  4. D Gray-line propagation
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Sporadic E

Why B is right. B. Sporadic E brings occasional strong signals on 10, 6 and 2 meters.

Sometimes small patches of the ionosphere's E region become dense enough to reflect signals on 10 meters, 6 meters and 2 meters. These patches form without warning and fade quickly, giving sudden strong signals from hundreds of miles away.

Why not C? D region absorption weakens signals; it does not bring them in.

T3C05

Which of the following effects may allow radio signals to travel beyond obstructions between the transmitting and receiving stations?

  1. A Knife-edge diffraction
  2. B Faraday rotation
  3. C Quantum tunneling
  4. D Doppler shift
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Knife-edge diffraction

Why A is right. A. Knife-edge diffraction lets signals bend around sharp obstructions.

When a radio wave passes a sharp edge, such as a ridge top or a roof line, part of it bends around the edge. This lets some signal reach a station hidden behind the obstruction.

Why not C? Quantum tunneling happens at the scale of atoms and does not carry radio signals past hills or buildings.

T3C06

What type of propagation is responsible for allowing over-the-horizon VHF and UHF communications to ranges of approximately 300 miles on a regular basis?

  1. A Tropospheric ducting
  2. B D region refraction
  3. C F2 region refraction
  4. D Faraday rotation
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Tropospheric ducting

Why A is right. A. Tropospheric ducting regularly carries VHF and UHF signals about 300 miles.

Layers in the lower atmosphere can trap VHF and UHF signals and guide them along like a pipe. This takes signals well past the normal horizon and is fairly common.

Why not C? F2 refraction mostly serves HF; VHF and UHF usually pass through the F2 region.

T3C07

What band is best suited for communicating via meteor scatter?

  1. A 33 centimeters
  2. B 6 meters
  3. C 2 meters
  4. D 70 centimeters
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 6 meters

Why B is right. B. 6 meters is best suited for meteor scatter.

A meteor burning up leaves a brief trail of charged gas high in the sky. Such trails reflect lower VHF signals best, and 6 meters gets the most from them. Higher bands are reflected more weakly and for shorter times.

Why not D? At 70 centimeters, meteor trails reflect very little, so contacts are rare.

T3C08

What causes tropospheric ducting?

  1. A Discharges of lightning during electrical storms
  2. B Sunspots and solar flares
  3. C Updrafts from hurricanes and tornadoes
  4. D Temperature inversions in the atmosphere
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Temperature inversions in the atmosphere

Why D is right. D. Temperature inversions cause tropospheric ducting.

Normally air gets cooler as you go up. In an inversion, a warm layer sits on top of cooler air. The sharp change bends VHF and UHF signals back down, trapping them in a duct that can carry them far.

Why not B? Sunspots and flares affect the ionosphere, not the lower atmosphere.

T3C09

What is generally the best time for long-distance 10-meter band propagation via the F region?

  1. A From dawn to shortly after sunset during periods of high sunspot activity
  2. B From shortly after sunset to dawn during periods of high sunspot activity
  3. C From dawn to shortly after sunset during periods of low sunspot activity
  4. D From shortly after sunset to dawn during periods of low sunspot activity
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. From dawn to shortly after sunset during periods of high sunspot activity

Why A is right. A. From dawn to shortly after sunset, during high sunspot activity.

Sunlight charges the F region. On 10 meters, the F region is dense enough to bend signals back only when the Sun is very active and the path is in daylight. After dark the charge fades, and the band usually closes.

Why not B? At night the F region weakens too much for 10 meters.

T3C10

Which of the following bands may provide long-distance communications via the ionosphere’s F region during the peak of the sunspot cycle?

  1. A 6 and 10 meters
  2. B 23 centimeters
  3. C 70 centimeters and 1.25 meters
  4. D All these choices are correct
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 6 and 10 meters

Why A is right. A. 6 and 10 meters can carry long-distance signals via the F region at the sunspot peak.

When the Sun is most active, the F region can bend signals as high as 6 meters back to Earth. Bands higher in frequency, such as 1.25 meters, 70 centimeters and 23 centimeters, still pass through it.

Why not D? Choices B and C are too high in frequency, so not all are correct.

T3C11

Why is the radio horizon for VHF and UHF signals more distant than the visual horizon?

  1. A Radio signals move somewhat faster than the speed of light
  2. B Radio waves are not blocked by dust particles
  3. C The atmosphere refracts radio waves slightly
  4. D Radio waves are blocked by dust particles
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. The atmosphere refracts radio waves slightly

Why C is right. C. The atmosphere bends radio waves slightly, carrying them past the visual horizon.

Air gets thinner with height, so waves travel a little faster higher up. This bends their path gently downward, following the curve of the Earth. The radio horizon ends up somewhat farther than the visual one.

Why not A? Radio waves cannot travel faster than light.
