G9A: Feed lines: characteristic impedance and attenuation; standing wave ratio (SWR) calculation, measurement, and effects; antenna feed point matching

Group G9A of the General pool (2023–2027) has 11 active questions. One of them is on every General exam. Each is shown word for word; open it to see the correct answer and our explanation.

G9A01

Which of the following factors determine the characteristic impedance of a parallel conductor feed line?

  1. A The distance between the centers of the conductors and the radius of the conductors
  2. B The distance between the centers of the conductors and the length of the line
  3. C The radius of the conductors and the frequency of the signal
  4. D The frequency of the signal and the length of the line
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. The distance between the centers of the conductors and the radius of the conductors

Why A is right. A. The line's impedance comes from its inductance and capacitance per foot, which are set mainly by how far apart and how thick the wires are.

Characteristic impedance is the ratio of voltage to current a signal sees as it travels down a line. Wider spacing or thinner wire raises it; the insulation between the wires also plays a part. Length and frequency do not change it.

Why not B? Line length changes loss, not characteristic impedance.

G9A02

What is the relationship between high standing wave ratio (SWR) and transmission line loss?

  1. A There is no relationship between transmission line loss and SWR
  2. B High SWR increases loss in a lossy transmission line
  3. C High SWR makes it difficult to measure transmission line loss
  4. D High SWR reduces the relative effect of transmission line loss
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. High SWR increases loss in a lossy transmission line

Why B is right. B. With a mismatch, power makes extra trips along the line and current peaks grow, so more of it turns to heat.

SWR, the standing wave ratio, measures how much power bounces back from a mismatch. Every trip through a lossy line turns some power into heat.

Why not D? High SWR makes the line's loss matter more, not less.

G9A03

What is the nominal characteristic impedance of “window line” transmission line?

  1. A 50 ohms
  2. B 75 ohms
  3. C 100 ohms
  4. D 450 ohms
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 450 ohms

Why D is right. D. Window line is about 450 ohms.

Window line is two parallel wires held apart by plastic with open slots cut out, like windows. The wide spacing gives it a high characteristic impedance, nominally 450 ohms. It has low loss even when the SWR is high.

Why not A? 50 ohms is typical of the coaxial cable most radios connect to.

G9A04

What causes reflected power at an antenna’s feed point?

  1. A Operating an antenna at its resonant frequency
  2. B Using more transmitter power than the antenna can handle
  3. C A difference between feed line impedance and antenna feed point impedance
  4. D Feeding the antenna with unbalanced feed line
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. A difference between feed line impedance and antenna feed point impedance

Why C is right. C. A line can hand all its power only to a load equal to its own impedance, so any difference sends part of it back.

Here the load is the antenna's feed point and the line is the feed line. The bigger the difference in impedance, the more power reflects.

Why not A? Operating at resonance tends to reduce reflection, not cause it.

G9A05

How does the attenuation of coaxial cable change with increasing frequency?

  1. A Attenuation is independent of frequency
  2. B Attenuation increases
  3. C Attenuation decreases
  4. D Attenuation follows Marconi’s Law of Attenuation
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Attenuation increases

Why B is right. B. Coax loses more of the signal as frequency goes up.

At higher frequencies, current crowds into a thinner layer at the surface of the conductors, which raises resistance. The insulation inside the cable also absorbs more energy. So a run of coax that works well on HF may waste much more power at UHF.

Why not C? Loss goes up with frequency, not down.

G9A06

In what units is RF feed line loss usually expressed?

  1. A Ohms per 1,000 feet
  2. B Decibels per 1,000 feet
  3. C Ohms per 100 feet
  4. D Decibels per 100 feet
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Decibels per 100 feet

Why D is right. D. Feed line loss is usually given in decibels per 100 feet.

Loss is a ratio of power out to power in, so it is stated in decibels, or dB. Makers list it for a standard 100-foot length at several frequencies. You can scale it for your own cable length.

Why not B? Decibels is right, but the usual standard length is 100 feet, not 1,000.

G9A07

What must be done to prevent standing waves on a feed line connected to an antenna?

  1. A The antenna feed point must be at DC ground potential
  2. B The feed line must be an odd number of electrical quarter wavelengths long
  3. C The feed line must be an even number of physical half wavelengths long
  4. D The antenna feed point impedance must be matched to the characteristic impedance of the feed line
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. The antenna feed point impedance must be matched to the characteristic impedance of the feed line

Why D is right. D. Standing waves need a reflection, and a load equal to the line's impedance absorbs everything, so nothing reflects.

Standing waves form when reflected power meets the power going forward. With a matched load, the SWR is 1:1.

Why not B? Line length changes what the transmitter sees, but it cannot remove the reflection at the antenna.

G9A08

If the SWR on an antenna feed line is 5:1, and a matching network at the transmitter end of the feed line is adjusted to present a 1:1 SWR to the transmitter, what is the resulting SWR on the feed line?

  1. A 1:1
  2. B 5:1
  3. C Between 1:1 and 5:1 depending on the characteristic impedance of the line
  4. D Between 1:1 and 5:1 depending on the reflected power at the transmitter
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 5:1

Why B is right. B. The SWR on the feed line stays 5:1.

The mismatch is at the antenna end, so reflections still travel along the whole line. A matching network at the transmitter only changes what the transmitter sees. It does not change anything on the line between the network and the antenna.

Why not A? 1:1 is what the transmitter sees after the network, not what is on the line.

G9A09

What standing wave ratio results from connecting a 50-ohm feed line to a 200-ohm resistive load?

  1. A 4:1
  2. B 1:4
  3. C 2:1
  4. D 1:2
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 4:1

Why A is right. A. A 200-ohm load on 50-ohm line gives an SWR of 4:1.

For a purely resistive load, SWR is the larger impedance divided by the smaller one. SWR is always written with the larger number first.

SWR = larger impedance ÷ smaller impedance
SWR = 200 ohms ÷ 50 ohms
SWR = 4, written 4:1
= 4:1

Why not B? 1:4 is written backward; SWR is always 1 or greater, so it is written 4:1.

G9A10

What standing wave ratio results from connecting a 50-ohm feed line to a 10-ohm resistive load?

  1. A 2:1
  2. B 1:2
  3. C 1:5
  4. D 5:1
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 5:1

Why D is right. D. A 10-ohm load on 50-ohm line gives an SWR of 5:1.

For a resistive load, divide the larger impedance by the smaller one. Here the line is the larger value. It does not matter whether the load is higher or lower than the line.

SWR = larger impedance ÷ smaller impedance
SWR = 50 ohms ÷ 10 ohms
SWR = 5, written 5:1
= 5:1

Why not C? 1:5 is written backward; SWR is always stated with the larger number first.

G9A11

What is the effect of transmission line loss on SWR measured at the input to the line?

  1. A Higher loss reduces SWR measured at the input to the line
  2. B Higher loss increases SWR measured at the input to the line
  3. C Higher loss increases the accuracy of SWR measured at the input to the line
  4. D Transmission line loss does not affect the SWR measurement
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Higher loss reduces SWR measured at the input to the line

Why A is right. A. Reflected power is weakened on its trip back down the line, so less of it reaches the meter.

Forward power also loses some before it reaches the antenna. With heavy loss, the SWR at the transmitter looks better than it really is at the antenna.

Why not B? Loss weakens the returning power, so it lowers the reading, not raises it.
