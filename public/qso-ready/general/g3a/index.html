G3A: Sunspots and solar radiation; geomagnetic field and stability indices

Group G3A of the General pool (2023–2027) has 14 active questions. One of them is on every General exam. Each is shown word for word; open it to see the correct answer and our explanation.

G3A01

How does a higher sunspot number affect HF propagation?

  1. A Higher sunspot numbers generally indicate a greater probability of good propagation at higher frequencies
  2. B Lower sunspot numbers generally indicate greater probability of sporadic E propagation
  3. C A zero sunspot number indicates that radio propagation is not possible on any band
  4. D A zero sunspot number indicates undisturbed conditions
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Higher sunspot numbers generally indicate a greater probability of good propagation at higher frequencies

Why A is right. A. More sunspots go with stronger solar ultraviolet light, which charges the upper atmosphere enough to bend higher frequencies back to Earth.

Sunspots mark active areas on the Sun that give off extra ultraviolet (UV) light. That UV knocks electrons loose from gas high above Earth, building the charged layers of the ionosphere. Denser layers can refract (bend) higher-frequency signals back down, so 15, 12 and 10 meters open more often when sunspot numbers are high. Low numbers do not shut radio down; the lower bands keep working.

Why not D? A low spot count says the Sun has few active areas; it does not promise that Earth's magnetic field is calm.

G3A02

What effect does a sudden ionospheric disturbance have on the daytime ionospheric propagation?

  1. A It enhances propagation on all HF frequencies
  2. B It disrupts signals on lower frequencies more than those on higher frequencies
  3. C It disrupts communications via satellite more than direct communications
  4. D None, because only areas on the night side of the Earth are affected
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. It disrupts signals on lower frequencies more than those on higher frequencies

Why B is right. B. Flare X-rays thicken the lowest, absorbing layer of the ionosphere, and that absorption hurts low frequencies the most.

A sudden ionospheric disturbance (SID) begins when X-rays from a solar flare hit the sunlit side of Earth. They charge the D region, the lowest layer, far more than normal. The D region absorbs radio energy, and the loss grows as frequency drops. So 80 and 40 meters can fade out while higher HF bands hold up better. The effect lasts from minutes to an hour or two.

Why not D? Flare radiation reaches only the side of Earth facing the Sun, so the day side is the side affected.

G3A03

Approximately how long does it take the increased ultraviolet and X-ray radiation from a solar flare to affect radio propagation on Earth?

  1. A 28 days
  2. B 1 to 2 hours
  3. C 8 minutes
  4. D 20 to 40 hours
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 8 minutes

Why C is right. C. Ultraviolet and X-rays are forms of light, and light crosses the distance from the Sun in a little over 8 minutes.

The Sun is about 150 million km away. Light covers that in roughly 500 seconds, which is about 8 minutes. So a flare's radio effects start at about the same moment the flare could first be seen. Clouds of charged particles from the Sun move much slower and arrive hours or days later.

Why not D? A delay of 20 to 40 hours fits slow-moving particle clouds, not light.

G3A04

Which of the following are the least reliable bands for long-distance communications during periods of low solar activity?

  1. A 80 meters and 160 meters
  2. B 60 meters and 40 meters
  3. C 30 meters and 20 meters
  4. D 15 meters, 12 meters, and 10 meters
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 15 meters, 12 meters, and 10 meters

Why D is right. D. The top HF bands need a heavily charged ionosphere to return their signals, and low solar activity does not provide it.

The higher the frequency, the more ionization it takes to bend a signal back to Earth. Near solar minimum, the maximum usable frequency (MUF), the highest frequency that returns over a path, often stays below 21 MHz. Then 15, 12 and 10 meters go quiet for long-distance work, while 80, 40 and 20 meters keep going.

Why not A? 80 and 160 meters are low enough to return even from a weak ionosphere, mainly at night.

G3A05

What is the solar flux index?

  1. A A measure of the highest frequency that is useful for ionospheric propagation between two points on Earth
  2. B A count of sunspots that is adjusted for solar emissions
  3. C Another name for the American sunspot number
  4. D A measure of solar radiation with a wavelength of 10.7 centimeters
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. A measure of solar radiation with a wavelength of 10.7 centimeters

Why D is right. D. The index is a daily reading of the Sun's radio output at 2800 MHz, a frequency whose wavelength is 10.7 cm.

Radio telescopes measure how much energy the Sun gives off at 2800 MHz. That number rises and falls with overall solar activity, so it is a handy stand-in for how much ionizing UV light reaches the upper atmosphere. Higher readings usually mean better conditions on the upper HF bands.

Why not B? The flux is measured with a radio receiver, not by counting spots.

G3A06

What is a geomagnetic storm?

  1. A A sudden drop in the solar flux index
  2. B A thunderstorm that affects radio propagation
  3. C Ripples in the geomagnetic force
  4. D A temporary disturbance in Earth’s geomagnetic field
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. A temporary disturbance in Earth’s geomagnetic field

Why D is right. D. It is a stretch of hours to days when Earth's magnetic field is knocked out of its normal, steady state and then recovers.

Bursts of charged particles from the Sun, such as a coronal mass ejection, can strike Earth's magnetic field. The field shifts and wobbles for hours or days, then settles back. During the storm the ionosphere is upset, mainly near the poles, and HF signals may fade or get noisy.

Why not B? Thunderstorms are weather in the lower air; a geomagnetic storm happens in Earth's magnetic field far above.

G3A07

At what point in the solar cycle does the 20-meter band usually support worldwide propagation during daylight hours?

  1. A At the summer solstice
  2. B Only at the maximum point
  3. C Only at the minimum point
  4. D At any point
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. At any point

Why D is right. D. 14 MHz needs only modest ionization, so the daytime ionosphere can return it in every part of the solar cycle.

During daylight, the F region is dense enough to bend 20-meter signals back to Earth even near solar minimum. Higher bands like 10 meters depend on high solar activity; 20 meters does not. That makes it a dependable band for daytime long-distance contacts all through the roughly 11-year sunspot cycle.

Why not B? Solar maximum helps, but daytime 20-meter openings do not depend on it.

G3A08

How can a geomagnetic storm affect HF propagation?

  1. A Improve high-latitude HF propagation
  2. B Degrade ground wave propagation
  3. C Improve ground wave propagation
  4. D Degrade high-latitude HF propagation
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Degrade high-latitude HF propagation

Why D is right. D. A storm drives extra particles into the polar regions, raising absorption there, so HF paths that cross high latitudes weaken or fail.

Earth's magnetic field steers charged particles toward the poles. During a geomagnetic storm, many more pour in, causing auroras and heavy absorption in the polar ionosphere. HF signals on routes near the poles, such as many paths from North America to Europe or Asia, can fade out. Ground wave travels along the surface and is not affected.

Why not C? Ground wave hugs Earth's surface, so a storm in the magnetic field does not change it.

G3A09

How can high geomagnetic activity benefit radio communications?

  1. A Creates auroras that can reflect VHF signals
  2. B Increases signal strength for HF signals passing through the polar regions
  3. C Improve HF long path propagation
  4. D Reduce long delayed echoes
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Creates auroras that can reflect VHF signals

Why A is right. A. The auroras that strong geomagnetic activity creates are charged regions high up that can bounce VHF signals to other stations.

When the magnetic field is stirred up, auroras glow in the sky near the poles. The charged air in an aurora can reflect signals at 50 MHz and above. Operators in the US point antennas north toward the aurora and bounce signals off it. These signals sound rough and buzzy because the reflecting area keeps moving.

Why not B? High activity weakens HF signals that cross the polar regions; it does not strengthen them.

G3A10

What causes HF propagation conditions to vary periodically in a 26- to 28-day cycle?

  1. A Long term oscillations in the upper atmosphere
  2. B Cyclic variation in Earth’s radiation belts
  3. C Rotation of the Sun’s surface layers around its axis
  4. D The position of the Moon in its orbit
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Rotation of the Sun’s surface layers around its axis

Why C is right. C. The Sun turns about once every 27 days as seen from Earth, so its active areas face us, rotate away and come back on that schedule.

The Sun is a ball of gas, and its surface turns once in roughly 26 to 28 days as seen from Earth. An active region or coronal hole aimed at Earth today will rotate out of view and return about four weeks later. Good or poor conditions tend to repeat on that cycle.

Why not D? The Moon does not control the charged layers of the ionosphere.

G3A11

How long does it take a coronal mass ejection to affect radio propagation on Earth?

  1. A 28 days
  2. B 14 days
  3. C 4 to 8 minutes
  4. D 15 hours to several days
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 15 hours to several days

Why D is right. D. A coronal mass ejection is a cloud of matter, which travels far slower than light, so it needs from about 15 hours to a few days.

A coronal mass ejection (CME) throws a huge cloud of charged gas off the Sun. Fast ones cross the 150 million km to Earth in under a day; slow ones take several days. When the cloud hits Earth's magnetic field, it can set off a geomagnetic storm and HF fadeouts. Flare light, by contrast, arrives in about 8 minutes.

Why not C? 4 to 8 minutes is about the travel time for light, not for a cloud of matter.

G3A12

What does the K-index measure?

  1. A The relative position of sunspots on the surface of the Sun
  2. B The short-term stability of Earth’s geomagnetic field
  3. C The short-term stability of the Sun’s magnetic field
  4. D The solar radio flux at Boulder, Colorado
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. The short-term stability of Earth’s geomagnetic field

Why B is right. B. The K-index rates how disturbed Earth's magnetic field has been over a short, three-hour window.

Instruments called magnetometers track changes in Earth's magnetic field. The K-index turns those changes into a number from 0 (calm) to 9 (severe storm), updated every three hours. A low K-index is good news for HF, especially on paths near the poles. A K of 5 or more counts as storm level.

Why not C? It measures Earth's field, not the Sun's.

G3A13

What does the A-index measure?

  1. A The relative position of sunspots on the surface of the Sun
  2. B The amount of polarization of the Sun’s electric field
  3. C The long-term stability of Earth’s geomagnetic field
  4. D The solar radio flux at Boulder, Colorado
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. The long-term stability of Earth’s geomagnetic field

Why C is right. C. The A-index sums up a full day of geomagnetic activity, so it shows the longer-term picture.

The A-index is built from a day's eight three-hour K readings, converted to a straight-line scale and averaged. It runs from 0 to 400. A low number means Earth's magnetic field stayed steady all day; a high number means a stormy day. Use K for right now and A for the day's trend.

Why not D? The Sun's radio output at 10.7 cm is the solar flux index, a different measurement.

G3A14

How is long distance radio communication usually affected by the charged particles that reach Earth from solar coronal holes?

  1. A HF communication is improved
  2. B HF communication is disturbed
  3. C VHF/UHF ducting is improved
  4. D VHF/UHF ducting is disturbed
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. HF communication is disturbed

Why B is right. B. Coronal holes send out fast streams of charged particles that can stir up Earth's magnetic field and upset HF paths.

A coronal hole is an area of the Sun's outer atmosphere where the magnetic field opens into space, letting the solar wind flow out faster. When a fast stream reaches Earth, it can raise the K and A indexes and weaken HF signals, mainly on high-latitude paths. Because the Sun rotates, a long-lasting hole can strike again about every 27 days.

Why not C? Ducting forms in the lower atmosphere from weather, not from solar particles.
