T1B: Frequency allocations; Emission modes; Spectrum sharing; Transmissions near band edges; Contacting the International Space Station; Power output

Group T1B of the Technician pool (2026–2030) has 12 active questions. One of them is on every Technician exam. Each is shown word for word; open it to see the correct answer and our explanation.

T1B01

Which of the following frequency ranges are available for phone operation by Technician licensees?

  1. A 28.050 MHz to 28.150 MHz
  2. B 28.100 MHz to 28.300 MHz
  3. C 28.300 MHz to 28.500 MHz
  4. D 28.500 MHz to 28.600 MHz
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 28.300 MHz to 28.500 MHz

Why C is right. C. Technician privileges on 10 meters end at 28.5 MHz, and the 97.305(c) table allows phone there only from 28.3 MHz up.

Technicians have 28.0-28.5 MHz on 10 meters under 97.301(e). The emission table splits that slice. From 28.0 to 28.3 MHz it allows RTTY and data, plus CW, which is allowed everywhere. From 28.3 to 28.5 MHz it allows phone, so that upper part is where a Technician may talk by voice on 10 meters.

“(xvii) 10 m | 28.0-28.3 MHz | RTTY, data … (xviii) 10 m | 28.3-28.5 MHz | Phone, image”

47 CFR 97.305(c)

Why not B? All of 28.100-28.300 MHz lies below 28.3 MHz, in the RTTY and data part, where phone is not allowed.

Remember: For Technicians on 10 meters, voice sits at 28.3 to 28.5.

T1B02

Which of the following U.S. amateur radio operators are allowed to contact the International Space Station (ISS) on VHF bands?

  1. A Only amateurs with a General class or higher license
  2. B Any amateur with a Technician class or higher license
  3. C Only amateurs with a General class or higher license, and NASA approval
  4. D Any amateurs with a Technician class or higher license, and NASA approval
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Any amateur with a Technician class or higher license

Why B is right. B. Any amateur with a Technician class or higher license.

A station on the ground that talks to a space station is an Earth station. Part 97 lets a holder of any license class be the control operator of an Earth station, within the privileges of that class. Technicians have the whole 2-meter band (144-148 MHz in the Americas), which includes the 144-146 MHz segment open to space stations like the ISS.

“A holder of any class operator license may be the control operator of an Earth station, subject to the privileges of the class of operator license held by the control operator.”

47 CFR 97.209(a)

Why not D? No Part 97 rule requires NASA approval; in practice, planned school contacts with the ISS are arranged through a separate program.

T1B03

Which frequency is in the 6-meter amateur band?

  1. A 49.00 MHz
  2. B 52.525 MHz
  3. C 28.50 MHz
  4. D 222.15 MHz
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 52.525 MHz

Why B is right. B. 52.525 MHz.

In the Americas (ITU Region 2), the 6-meter band runs from 50 to 54 MHz, and Technicians have the whole band. 52.525 MHz falls inside it. 49.00 MHz is below the band, 28.50 MHz is in 10 meters, and 222.15 MHz is in the 1.25-meter band.

“6 m | | 50-54 | 50-54”

47 CFR 97.301(a)

Why not A? 49.00 MHz is 1 MHz below where the 6-meter band starts.

Remember: Six meters: fifty to fifty-four.

T1B04

Which amateur band includes 146.52 MHz?

  1. A 6 meters
  2. B 20 meters
  3. C 70 centimeters
  4. D 2 meters
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 2 meters

Why D is right. D. 146.52 MHz sits inside the 144-148 MHz band, and 300 ÷ 146.52 is about 2 meters.

Band names come from wavelength. Divide 300 by the frequency in MHz to get the wavelength in meters. For 146.52 MHz that gives about 2 meters. The table lists the 2-meter band as 144-148 MHz in the Americas, and 146.52 MHz is inside it.

wavelength (m) = 300 / frequency (MHz)
wavelength = 300 / 146.52
wavelength ≈ 2.05 m
= 2.05 m

“2 m | 144-146 | 144-148 | 144-148”

47 CFR 97.301(a)

Why not A? 6 meters is near 50 MHz, about one-third of 146.52 MHz.

T1B05

Which of the following bands include frequencies where Technicians are authorized to use digital modes such as FT8?

  1. A 10 meters
  2. B 6 meters
  3. C 2 meters
  4. D All these choices are correct
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. All these choices are correct

Why D is right. D. All these choices are correct.

FT8 is a data mode. Technicians have 28.0-28.5 MHz on 10 meters and every band from 6 meters up. The emission table allows data on 28.0-28.3 MHz, on 6 meters from 50.1 MHz up, and on 2 meters from 144.1 MHz up. So all three bands have spots where a Technician may use FT8.

“(xvii) 10 m | 28.0-28.3 MHz | RTTY, data … (i) 6 m | 50.1-51.0 MHz | MCW, phone, image, RTTY, data … (iii) 2 m | 144.1-148.0 MHz | MCW, phone, image, RTTY, data, test”

47 CFR 97.305(c)

Why not A? 10 meters is right, but 6 and 2 meters also allow data, so one band alone is too narrow.

T1B06

On which HF bands does a Technician class operator have phone privileges?

  1. A None
  2. B 10-meter band only
  3. C 80-meter, 40-meter, 15-meter, and 10-meter bands
  4. D 30-meter band only
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 10-meter band only

Why B is right. B. 10-meter band only.

Technicians have HF segments on 80, 40, 15 and 10 meters. On 80, 40 and 15 meters, the rule limits a Technician to CW (Morse code). On 10 meters, the emission table allows phone from 28.3 to 28.5 MHz. So 10 meters is the only HF band where a Technician may use voice.

“A station having a control operator holding a Novice or Technician Class operator license may only transmit a CW emission using the international Morse code.”

47 CFR 97.307(f)(9)

Why not C? On 80, 40 and 15 meters a Technician may send only CW, not voice.

T1B07

Which of the following VHF/UHF band segments are limited to CW only?

  1. A 50.0 MHz to 50.1 MHz and 144.0 MHz to 144.1 MHz
  2. B 219 MHz to 220 MHz and 420.0 MHz to 420.1 MHz
  3. C 902.0 MHz to 902.1 MHz
  4. D All these choices are correct
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 50.0 MHz to 50.1 MHz and 144.0 MHz to 144.1 MHz

Why A is right. A. The 97.305(c) table starts every other mode 100 kHz above the bottom of 6 and 2 meters, leaving only CW, which 97.305(a) allows anywhere.

CW is allowed on any frequency an operator may use. The emission table authorizes other modes on 6 meters only from 50.1 MHz up and on 2 meters only from 144.1 MHz up. So on 50.0-50.1 MHz and 144.0-144.1 MHz, CW is the only mode the table allows.

“(i) 6 m | 50.1-51.0 MHz | MCW, phone, image, RTTY, data … (iii) 2 m | 144.1-148.0 MHz | MCW, phone, image, RTTY, data, test”

47 CFR 97.305(c)

Why not B? 219-220 MHz is listed for data, and the table allows phone and other modes on the entire 70 cm band, including 420.0-420.1 MHz.

Remember: Other modes start 100 kHz up on 6 and 2 meters.

T1B08

How are US amateurs restricted in segments of bands where the Amateur Radio Service is secondary?

  1. A U.S. amateurs may find non-amateur stations in those segments, and must avoid interfering with them
  2. B U.S. amateurs must give foreign amateur stations priority in those segments
  3. C International communications are not permitted in those segments
  4. D Digital transmissions are not permitted in those segments
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. U.S. amateurs may find non-amateur stations in those segments, and must avoid interfering with them

Why A is right. A. U.S. amateurs may find non-amateur stations in those segments, and must avoid interfering with them.

In some bands hams are a secondary user. Another service, such as government radar, has first claim. A secondary station must not cause harmful interference to the primary service and must put up with interference from it. You share the band, but you yield.

“A station in a secondary service must not cause harmful interference to, and must accept interference from, stations in a primary service.”

47 CFR 97.303

Why not B? Secondary status is about other services, not about foreign amateurs.

Remember: Secondary means guest: keep quiet and don't complain about the host.

T1B09

Why should you not set your transmit frequency to be exactly at the edge of an amateur band or sub-band?

  1. A To allow for calibration error in the transmitter frequency display
  2. B So that modulation sidebands do not extend beyond the band edge
  3. C To allow for transmitter frequency drift
  4. D All these choices are correct
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. All these choices are correct

Why D is right. D. Sidebands, dial calibration error and drift can each push part of your signal past the edge, so all three are reasons to stay inside it.

Your signal is not a single point. Voice and data spread out from the dial frequency, to one side or both depending on the mode, the dial may read a little off, and the transmitter can drift as it warms up. Any of these can push part of your signal past the band edge. Good practice is to stay a few kHz inside.

“each amateur station must be operated in accordance with good engineering and good amateur practice”

47 CFR 97.101(a)

T1B10

Where may SSB phone be used in amateur bands above 50 MHz?

  1. A Only in sub-bands allocated to General class or higher licensees
  2. B Only on repeaters
  3. C In at least some segment of all these bands
  4. D On any band if the power is limited to 25 watts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. In at least some segment of all these bands

Why C is right. C. In at least some segment of all these bands.

Single sideband (SSB) is a voice (phone) mode. Technicians may use every band from 6 meters up. The emission table lists phone in at least part of each of those bands: 6 meters from 50.1 MHz, 2 meters from 144.1 MHz, 1.25 meters at 222-225 MHz, and the entire band from 70 cm up. No rule limits SSB to repeaters or to 25 watts.

“(i) 6 m | 50.1-51.0 MHz | MCW, phone … (iii) 2 m | 144.1-148.0 MHz | MCW, phone … (i) 70 cm | Entire band | MCW, phone”

47 CFR 97.305(c)

Why not B? The table allows phone well beyond repeater channels, so SSB is not limited to repeaters.

T1B11

What is the maximum peak envelope power output for Technician class operators in their HF band segments?

  1. A 200 watts
  2. B 100 watts
  3. C 50 watts
  4. D 10 watts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 200 watts

Why A is right. A. 200 watts.

PEP means peak envelope power, the highest power your transmitter puts out on its strongest peaks. On the HF segments Technicians may use (80, 40, 15 and 10 meters), the rule caps Technician output at 200 W PEP.

“No station may transmit with a transmitter power output exceeding 200 W PEP… 28.0-28.5 MHz segment when the control operator is a Novice Class operator or a Technician Class operator”

47 CFR 97.313(c)(2)

Why not B? 100 watts is a common radio rating, but the rule limit is 200 W PEP.

T1B12

Except for some specific restrictions, what is the maximum peak envelope power output for Technician class operators using frequencies above 30 MHz?

  1. A 50 watts
  2. B 100 watts
  3. C 500 watts
  4. D 1500 watts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 1500 watts

Why D is right. D. 1500 watts.

Above 30 MHz, Technicians follow the same general power cap as all classes: 1.5 kW PEP, which is 1500 watts. A few bands and areas have lower limits. The rules also say to use the least power needed for the contact.

“No station may transmit with a transmitter power exceeding 1.5 kW PEP.”

47 CFR 97.313(b)

Why not C? 500 watts is not a Part 97 limit for Technicians above 30 MHz.

Remember: 1.5 kW is the same as 1500 W.
