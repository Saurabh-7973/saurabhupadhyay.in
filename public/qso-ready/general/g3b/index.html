G3B: Maximum Usable Frequency; Lowest Usable Frequency; short path and long path propagation; determining propagation conditions; ionospheric refraction

Group G3B of the General pool (2023–2027) has 12 active questions. One of them is on every General exam. Each is shown word for word; open it to see the correct answer and our explanation.

G3B01

What is a characteristic of skywave signals arriving at your location by both short-path and long-path propagation?

  1. A Periodic fading approximately every 10 seconds
  2. B Signal strength increased by 3 dB
  3. C The signal might be cancelled causing severe attenuation
  4. D A slightly delayed echo might be heard
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. A slightly delayed echo might be heard

Why D is right. D. The long-path copy has much farther to go, so it reaches you a fraction of a second after the short-path copy.

A signal can reach you two ways around Earth: the short way and the long way. The long way can be many thousands of kilometers longer. Radio needs about 0.13 second to circle Earth once, so the long-path copy lags behind. Hearing both at once gives a faint echo after each sound.

Why not C? The two copies arrive at different times, so you hear an echo rather than one signal steadily canceled.

G3B02

What factors affect the MUF?

  1. A Path distance and location
  2. B Time of day and season
  3. C Solar radiation and ionospheric disturbances
  4. D All these choices are correct
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. All these choices are correct

Why D is right. D. The MUF depends on how strongly the ionosphere is charged along the path, and each listed factor changes that.

The maximum usable frequency (MUF) is the highest frequency that will bend back to Earth over a given path. It depends on how long the path is and where it runs, on the hour and season (how much sunlight that part of the sky gets), and on solar output and disturbances. Change any one and the MUF moves.

G3B03

Which frequency will have the least attenuation for long-distance skip propagation?

  1. A Just below the MUF
  2. B Just above the LUF
  3. C Just below the critical frequency
  4. D Just above the critical frequency
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Just below the MUF

Why A is right. A. Absorption drops as frequency rises, so the highest frequency that still returns over the path loses the least energy.

The D region, the lowest layer, absorbs signals, and it absorbs low frequencies the most. Among frequencies that will still bend back over your path, the top one passes through that layer with the smallest loss. Go above the maximum usable frequency (MUF) and the signal escapes into space instead.

Why not B? Near the lowest usable frequency, absorption is at its worst.

G3B04

Which of the following is a way to determine current propagation on a desired band from your station?

  1. A Use a network of automated receiving stations on the internet to see where your transmissions are being received
  2. B Check the A-index
  3. C Send a series of dots and listen for echoes
  4. D All these choices are correct
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Use a network of automated receiving stations on the internet to see where your transmissions are being received

Why A is right. A. Automated receiver networks report back where your own signal was actually decoded, giving a direct check on your paths right now.

Online reporting networks link receivers around the world that listen and post what they hear, with the time and signal strength. Call CQ or send a test on the band, then check the map to see which areas heard you. General numbers like the A-index describe overall conditions, not your own station's reach.

Why not C? Listening for echoes of your own dots tells you little about where your signal can reach.

G3B05

How does the ionosphere affect radio waves with frequencies below the MUF and above the LUF?

  1. A They are refracted back to Earth
  2. B They pass through the ionosphere
  3. C They are amplified by interaction with the ionosphere
  4. D They are refracted and trapped in the ionosphere to circle Earth
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. They are refracted back to Earth

Why A is right. A. Between the two limits, the ionosphere bends signals back down without heavy loss, which is why that span of frequencies works.

Above the maximum usable frequency (MUF), signals pass through the ionosphere and are lost to space. Below the lowest usable frequency (LUF), the D region absorbs too much of them. In between, the F region refracts (bends) them back toward Earth, so they reach the far station with useful strength.

Why not B? Passing through the ionosphere happens above the MUF.

G3B06

What usually happens to radio waves with frequencies below the LUF?

  1. A They are refracted back to Earth
  2. B They pass through the ionosphere
  3. C They are attenuated before reaching the destination
  4. D They are refracted and trapped in the ionosphere to circle Earth
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. They are attenuated before reaching the destination

Why C is right. C. Below the LUF, absorption in the low ionosphere weakens the signal until it is too faint to use at the far end.

The lowest usable frequency (LUF) is the lowest frequency that arrives over a path with enough strength to copy. Lower frequencies lose more energy in the D region, the lowest layer of the ionosphere. Below the LUF, that loss, along with noise at the receiver, leaves the signal unusable.

Why not B? Passing through the ionosphere is what happens above the MUF, not below the LUF.

G3B07

What does LUF stand for?

  1. A The Lowest Usable Frequency for communications between two specific points
  2. B The Lowest Usable Frequency for communications to any point outside a 100-mile radius
  3. C The Lowest Usable Frequency during a 24-hour period
  4. D The Lowest Usable Frequency during the past 60 minutes
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. The Lowest Usable Frequency for communications between two specific points

Why A is right. A. The LUF is tied to one particular path, from a given station to another given station.

The lowest usable frequency (LUF) is the lowest frequency that gets through over a path with useful strength. It depends on both end points, because absorption and noise change with distance and route. It also changes through the day. No fixed radius or time window is part of the definition.

G3B08

What does MUF stand for?

  1. A The Minimum Usable Frequency for communications between two points
  2. B The Maximum Usable Frequency for communications between two points
  3. C The Minimum Usable Frequency during a 24-hour period
  4. D The Maximum Usable Frequency during a 24-hour period
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. The Maximum Usable Frequency for communications between two points

Why B is right. B. The term names the top frequency the ionosphere will return over one particular path.

The maximum usable frequency (MUF) is the ceiling for a skywave path. Above it, signals pass through the ionosphere and are lost to space. Because it depends on the path, two points 1,000 km apart have a different MUF than two points 3,000 km apart, at the same moment.

Why not A? The lower limit is the LUF, and the word in it is lowest, not minimum.

G3B09

What is the approximate maximum distance along the Earth’s surface normally covered in one hop using the F2 region?

  1. A 180 miles
  2. B 1,200 miles
  3. C 2,500 miles
  4. D 12,000 miles
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 2,500 miles

Why C is right. C. The F2 region is the highest layer, roughly 300 km up, and that height lets a single hop reach about 2,500 miles.

How far one skywave hop goes depends on how high the bending layer is. A signal that leaves near the horizon and turns back at about 300 to 400 km lands roughly 4,000 km (2,500 miles) away. Earth's curve stops a single hop from going much farther, so longer paths need two or more hops.

Why not B? About 1,200 miles is the one-hop limit for the lower E region.

G3B10

What is the approximate maximum distance along the Earth’s surface normally covered in one hop using the E region?

  1. A 180 miles
  2. B 1,200 miles
  3. C 2,500 miles
  4. D 12,000 miles
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 1,200 miles

Why B is right. B. The E region sits only about 100 km up, so a single hop from it lands no more than about 1,200 miles away.

A lower bending layer gives a shorter hop. The E region is about 100 to 120 km high. A signal that leaves near the horizon and turns back there returns to Earth about 2,000 km (1,200 miles) away. Sporadic E openings on 6 and 10 meters often cover about this distance.

Why not C? 2,500 miles takes the higher F2 region.

G3B11

What happens to HF propagation when the LUF exceeds the MUF?

  1. A Propagation via ordinary skywave communications is not possible over that path
  2. B HF communications over the path are enhanced
  3. C Double-hop propagation along the path is more common
  4. D Propagation over the path on all HF frequencies is enhanced
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Propagation via ordinary skywave communications is not possible over that path

Why A is right. A. If the lowest frequency that gets through is higher than the highest one that bends back, no frequency is left for normal skywave on that path.

The lowest usable frequency (LUF) is set by absorption. The maximum usable frequency (MUF) is set by how much the ionosphere can bend signals. Normally the LUF is below the MUF, leaving a usable window. If the LUF climbs above the MUF, as during strong absorption events, the window closes.

Why not B? Losing the usable window makes the path worse, not better.

G3B12

Which of the following is typical of the lower HF frequencies during the summer?

  1. A Poor propagation at any time of day
  2. B World-wide propagation during daylight hours
  3. C Heavy distortion on signals due to photon absorption
  4. D High levels of atmospheric noise or static
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. High levels of atmospheric noise or static

Why D is right. D. Summer thunderstorms fill the low HF bands with static crashes, because lightning noise is strongest at lower frequencies.

Each lightning strike sends out a burst of radio noise that can travel hundreds of miles or more. The noise is loudest at lower frequencies, so 160, 80 and 40 meters get heavy static in summer. Along with stronger daytime absorption, this makes weak signals hard to copy on the low bands.

Why not B? In daylight the low bands are mostly short range, because the D region absorbs their signals.
