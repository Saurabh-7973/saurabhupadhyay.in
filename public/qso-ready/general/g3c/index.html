G3C: Ionospheric regions; critical angle and frequency; HF scatter; near vertical incidence skywave (NVIS)

Group G3C of the General pool (2023–2027) has 11 active questions. One of them is on every General exam. Each is shown word for word; open it to see the correct answer and our explanation.

G3C01

Which ionospheric region is closest to the surface of Earth?

  1. A The D region
  2. B The E region
  3. C The F1 region
  4. D The F2 region
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. The D region

Why A is right. A. The D region is the lowest layer of the ionosphere, starting around 60 km up.

From the ground up, the layers are D (about 60 to 90 km), E (about 90 to 150 km), then F1 and F2, with F2 highest. The D region forms mainly in daylight and fades after dark. It absorbs signals more than it bends them, especially at lower frequencies.

Why not D? F2 is the highest region, not the lowest.

Remember: D for down low: the first layer above your head.

G3C02

What is meant by the term “critical frequency” at a given incidence angle?

  1. A The highest frequency which is refracted back to Earth
  2. B The lowest frequency which is refracted back to Earth
  3. C The frequency at which the signal-to-noise ratio approaches unity
  4. D The frequency at which the signal-to-noise ratio is 6 dB
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. The highest frequency which is refracted back to Earth

Why A is right. A. Critical frequency is the upper limit: above it, a wave at that angle goes through the layer instead of coming back.

Each layer of the ionosphere can bend radio waves back only up to a certain frequency. That limit depends on how dense the layer is and on the angle at which the wave strikes it. For a given angle, the highest frequency still returned is the critical frequency. Measured straight up, it shows how strong a layer is.

Why not B? The low-frequency limit on a path comes from absorption, not from refraction.

G3C03

Why is skip propagation via the F2 region longer than that via the other ionospheric regions?

  1. A Because it is the densest
  2. B Because of the Doppler effect
  3. C Because it is the highest
  4. D Because of temperature inversions
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Because it is the highest

Why C is right. C. The higher the turning point, the farther a signal travels before coming down, and F2 is the highest layer.

Picture a signal leaving at a low angle. The higher the point where it bends back, the farther away it lands. The F2 region is about 250 to 400 km up, well above the E region at about 100 km. So F2 hops are much longer: about 2,500 miles compared with about 1,200 miles.

Why not A? Density decides which frequencies come back, not how far each hop goes.

G3C04

What does the term “critical angle” mean, as applied to radio wave propagation?

  1. A The long path azimuth of a distant station
  2. B The short path azimuth of a distant station
  3. C The lowest takeoff angle that will return a radio wave to Earth under specific ionospheric conditions
  4. D The highest takeoff angle that will return a radio wave to Earth under specific ionospheric conditions
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. The highest takeoff angle that will return a radio wave to Earth under specific ionospheric conditions

Why D is right. D. Waves sent up more steeply than the critical angle push through the ionosphere, so this angle marks the upper limit for a return.

Takeoff angle is how high above the horizon a wave leaves the antenna. A low wave meets the ionosphere at a glancing angle and bends back easily. As the angle rises, the wave hits more head-on and needs more bending. At some angle, for that frequency and those conditions, it breaks through. That limit is the critical angle.

Why not C? Low angles return most easily, so the limit is at the high end, not the low end.

G3C05

Why is long-distance communication on the 40-, 60-, 80-, and 160-meter bands more difficult during the day?

  1. A The F region absorbs signals at these frequencies during daylight hours
  2. B The F region is unstable during daylight hours
  3. C The D region absorbs signals at these frequencies during daylight hours
  4. D The E region is unstable during daylight hours
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. The D region absorbs signals at these frequencies during daylight hours

Why C is right. C. Sunlight builds up the D region in the daytime, and it soaks up energy at these lower frequencies.

The D region, the lowest layer, forms when sunlight strikes the upper air. It does not bend HF signals back; it absorbs them, and the loss is heaviest below about 10 MHz. Daytime signals on 160, 80, 60 and 40 meters cross it twice on each hop, so long-distance contacts fade. After sunset the D region weakens and these bands open up.

Why not A? The F region bends signals back to Earth rather than absorbing them.

G3C06

What is a characteristic of HF scatter?

  1. A Phone signals have high intelligibility
  2. B Signals have a fluttering sound
  3. C There are very large, sudden swings in signal strength
  4. D Scatter propagation occurs only at night
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Signals have a fluttering sound

Why B is right. B. Scatter signals arrive by many routes of slightly different lengths, so they waver quickly and sound fluttery.

In HF scatter, small parts of a signal bounce off uneven patches in the ionosphere or on the ground and reach stations that normal skip would miss. Because each piece takes a different route, the pieces drift in and out of step many times a second, giving a quick, shallow flutter. The result is a weak, wavering sound that can make speech hard to follow.

Why not A? The wavering and blurring lower intelligibility on phone.

G3C07

What makes HF scatter signals often sound distorted?

  1. A The ionospheric region involved is unstable
  2. B Ground waves are absorbing much of the signal
  3. C The E region is not present
  4. D Energy is scattered into the skip zone through several different paths
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Energy is scattered into the skip zone through several different paths

Why D is right. D. Bits of the signal reach you over several routes of different lengths and blend together, which smears the sound.

The skip zone is the gap between where ground wave fades out and where the first skywave hop lands. Scatter fills that gap weakly by sending bits of energy from many spots. Each bit arrives with a slightly different delay, and the delayed copies blur together, so speech sounds distorted.

Why not C? Whether the E region is present is not what blurs scatter signals.

G3C08

Why are HF scatter signals in the skip zone usually weak?

  1. A Only a small part of the signal energy is scattered into the skip zone
  2. B Signals are scattered from the magnetosphere, which is not a good reflector
  3. C Propagation is via ground waves, which absorb most of the signal energy
  4. D Propagation is via ducts in the F region, which absorb most of the energy
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Only a small part of the signal energy is scattered into the skip zone

Why A is right. A. Scatter sends only a small share of the transmitted power into the skip zone, so what arrives there is faint.

Most of a skywave signal follows the normal path and lands beyond the skip zone. Uneven patches in the ionosphere, or rough ground where the hop lands, spray a little energy in other directions. Only that small share reaches stations inside the skip zone.

Why not C? Ground wave has already faded out by the skip zone, which is why the zone is a gap.

G3C09

What type of propagation allows signals to be heard in the transmitting station’s skip zone?

  1. A Faraday rotation
  2. B Scatter
  3. C Chordal hop
  4. D Short-path
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Scatter

Why B is right. B. Scattering spreads small amounts of energy in many directions, including back into the area that normal skip jumps over.

The skip zone is the area too far for ground wave and too close for the first skywave hop. Normally you hear nothing there. Scatter, from rough patches in the ionosphere or from the ground where a hop lands, sends some energy back into that gap, so weak signals can be heard.

Why not D? Short-path only names which way around Earth a signal goes; it does not put energy into the skip zone.

G3C10

What is near vertical incidence skywave (NVIS) propagation?

  1. A Propagation near the MUF
  2. B Short distance MF or HF propagation at high elevation angles
  3. C Long path HF propagation at sunrise and sunset
  4. D Double hop propagation near the LUF
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Short distance MF or HF propagation at high elevation angles

Why B is right. B. NVIS sends signals almost straight up so they come back down nearby, covering the area around the station.

Near vertical incidence skywave (NVIS) uses an antenna mounted low, often a dipole a small fraction of a wavelength high, that sends most energy upward. The ionosphere returns it in a circle around the station out to a few hundred miles, with no skip zone. It works on lower bands such as 80, 60 and 40 meters, and suits regional emergency nets.

Why not A? NVIS needs frequencies low enough to return from straight overhead, usually well below the MUF for longer paths.

G3C11

Which ionospheric region is the most absorbent of signals below 10 MHz during daylight hours?

  1. A The F2 region
  2. B The F1 region
  3. C The E region
  4. D The D region
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. The D region

Why D is right. D. In daylight, the D region absorbs signals below about 10 MHz more than any other layer.

The D region is the lowest layer, about 60 to 90 km up. Its air is dense enough that freed electrons often bump into gas molecules, turning radio energy into heat. That loss is strongest at lower frequencies and happens mainly in daylight. The higher F regions bend signals back instead of absorbing them.

Why not A? F2 bends signals back to Earth rather than absorbing them.
