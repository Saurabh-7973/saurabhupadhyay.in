G2B: Operating effectively; band plans; drills and emergencies; RACES operation

Group G2B of the General pool (2023–2027) has 11 active questions. One of them is on every General exam. Each is shown word for word; open it to see the correct answer and our explanation.

G2B01

Which of the following is true concerning access to frequencies?

  1. A Nets have priority
  2. B QSOs in progress have priority
  3. C Except during emergencies, no amateur station has priority access to any frequency
  4. D Contest operations should yield to non-contest use of frequencies
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Except during emergencies, no amateur station has priority access to any frequency

Why C is right. C. The bands are shared, and the rules give priority only to emergency communications.

Part 97 says no station is given a frequency for its own use, and all operators must cooperate in sharing the bands. The one exception is emergencies: control operators must give way to stations handling emergency traffic. Nets, contacts in progress and contests have no legal claim to a frequency; courtesy decides who moves.

“No frequency will be assigned for the exclusive use of any station.”

47 CFR 97.101(b)

Why not A? A net's habit of meeting on a frequency gives it no legal priority there.

G2B02

What is the first thing you should do if you are communicating with another amateur station and hear a station in distress break in?

  1. A Inform your local emergency coordinator
  2. B Acknowledge the station in distress and determine what assistance may be needed
  3. C Immediately decrease power to avoid interfering with the station in distress
  4. D Immediately cease all transmissions
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Acknowledge the station in distress and determine what assistance may be needed

Why B is right. B. Your first job is to let the distressed station know it was heard and to learn what is wrong.

A station in distress comes before any routine contact. Stop your conversation, answer the station, and find out where it is and what help it needs. Then you can pass the information to the right authorities.

“each control operator must give priority to stations providing emergency communications”

47 CFR 97.101(c)

Why not D? Going silent leaves the station in distress with no one to help it.

G2B03

What is good amateur practice if propagation changes during a contact creating interference from other stations using the frequency?

  1. A Advise the interfering stations that you are on the frequency and that you have priority
  2. B Decrease power and continue to transmit
  3. C Attempt to resolve the interference problem with the other stations in a mutually acceptable manner
  4. D Switch to the opposite sideband
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Attempt to resolve the interference problem with the other stations in a mutually acceptable manner

Why C is right. C. No one owns a frequency, so the stations involved should work out a fix that suits everyone.

Band conditions change during the day, and stations that could not hear each other at first may start to overlap. Neither side has a right to the frequency. Talk it over: one group might move a few kHz, or you might take turns. Polite cooperation keeps the band usable for all.

Why not A? Except for emergency traffic, no station has priority on a frequency.

G2B04

When selecting a CW transmitting frequency, what minimum separation from other stations should be used to minimize interference to stations on adjacent frequencies?

  1. A 5 Hz to 50 Hz
  2. B 150 Hz to 500 Hz
  3. C 1 kHz to 3 kHz
  4. D 3 kHz to 6 kHz
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. 150 Hz to 500 Hz

Why B is right. B. CW signals are very narrow, so a few hundred hertz of spacing keeps neighbors clear of each other.

A clean CW signal at common speeds is roughly 100 Hz wide. Staying 150 to 500 Hz from the nearest station keeps you clear of its signal while using little of the band. Use the wider end of that span when signals are strong.

Why not C? 1 to 3 kHz is about the room a voice signal needs, which wastes space on CW.

G2B05

When selecting an SSB transmitting frequency, what minimum separation should be used to minimize interference to stations on adjacent frequencies?

  1. A 5 Hz to 50 Hz
  2. B 150 Hz to 500 Hz
  3. C 2 kHz to 3 kHz
  4. D Approximately 6 kHz
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 2 kHz to 3 kHz

Why C is right. C. An SSB voice signal is about 2.4 to 3 kHz wide, so the next station needs that much room to stay clear.

Single sideband (SSB) voice takes up roughly 2.4 to 3 kHz. If you set up closer than that to another SSB station, your signals overlap and both sides hear each other. Allowing 2 to 3 kHz of spacing is the usual minimum on a busy band.

Why not B? 150 to 500 Hz suits narrow CW signals, not voice.

G2B06

How can you avoid harmful interference on an apparently clear frequency before calling CQ on CW or phone?

  1. A Send “QRL?” on CW, followed by your call sign; or, if using phone, ask if the frequency is in use, followed by your call sign
  2. B Listen for 2 minutes before calling CQ
  3. C Send the letter “V” in Morse code several times and listen for a response, or say “test” several times and listen for a response
  4. D Send “QSY” on CW or if using phone, announce “the frequency is in use,” then give your call sign and listen for a response
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Send “QRL?” on CW, followed by your call sign; or, if using phone, ask if the frequency is in use, followed by your call sign

Why A is right. A. Asking first gives anyone already using the frequency a chance to say so before you start calling.

A frequency can sound empty when you hear only one side of a contact, or when a station is pausing. On CW, send QRL? (is this frequency in use?) with your call sign. On phone, ask out loud and give your call sign. Listen, and if anyone answers, move.

Why not D? QSY means change frequency, so it does not ask whether the frequency is busy.

G2B07

Which of the following complies with commonly accepted amateur practice when choosing a frequency on which to initiate a call?

  1. A Listen on the frequency for at least two minutes to be sure it is clear
  2. B Identify your station by transmitting your call sign at least 3 times
  3. C Follow the voluntary band plan
  4. D All these choices are correct
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Follow the voluntary band plan

Why C is right. C. Band plans are voluntary agreements on where each kind of activity goes, and following them keeps activities from clashing.

Part 97 sets broad limits by license class and mode. Inside those limits, amateurs agree on band plans that set aside parts of each band for CW, digital modes, SSB, beacons, DX windows and more. Starting a call in the right part of the band helps avoid interfering with others.

Why not B? Sending your call sign three times is not a custom for choosing a frequency.

G2B08

What is the voluntary band plan restriction for US stations transmitting within the 48 contiguous states in the 50.1 MHz to 50.125 MHz band segment?

  1. A Only contacts with stations not within the 48 contiguous states
  2. B Only contacts with other stations within the 48 contiguous states
  3. C Only digital contacts
  4. D Only SSTV contacts
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Only contacts with stations not within the 48 contiguous states

Why A is right. A. This small slice of 6 meters is a DX window, so US stations in the lower 48 use it only to work stations outside those states.

On 6 meters, signals from far away are often weak and short-lived. The voluntary band plan keeps 50.100 to 50.125 MHz free of local chatter so those distant signals can be heard. Contacts between stations inside the 48 contiguous states belong elsewhere on the band.

Why not B? Contacts between stations in the lower 48 are what the window asks you to avoid.

G2B09

Who may be the control operator of an amateur station transmitting in RACES to assist relief operations during a disaster?

  1. A Only a person holding an FCC-issued amateur operator license
  2. B Only a RACES net control operator
  3. C A person holding an FCC-issued amateur operator license or an appropriate government official
  4. D Any control operator when normal communication systems are operational
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Only a person holding an FCC-issued amateur operator license

Why A is right. A. RACES stations are amateur stations, so their control operators must hold FCC amateur licenses.

RACES (Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service) lets amateurs support government emergency managers. Under Part 97, the control operator must hold an FCC-issued amateur license and be enrolled with a civil defense organization. A government official without an amateur license cannot serve as control operator, even during a disaster.

“No person may be the control operator of an amateur station transmitting in RACES unless that person holds a FCC-issued amateur operator license”

47 CFR 97.407(a)

Why not C? A government official needs an amateur license of their own to be the control operator.

G2B10

Which of the following is good amateur practice for net management?

  1. A Always use multiple sets of phonetics during check-in
  2. B Have a backup frequency in case of interference or poor conditions
  3. C Transmit the full net roster at the beginning of every session
  4. D All these choices are correct
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Have a backup frequency in case of interference or poor conditions

Why B is right. B. A backup frequency lets the net keep going if the main one becomes unusable.

Interference, fading or a repeater failure can cut a net short. Choosing a second frequency ahead of time, and telling members about it, means everyone knows where to regroup. Long roster readings and extra phonetics slow a net down without solving that problem.

Why not A? Repeating several phonetic alphabets wastes time; one standard set is enough.

G2B11

How often may RACES training drills and tests be routinely conducted without special authorization?

  1. A No more than 1 hour per month
  2. B No more than 2 hours per month
  3. C No more than 1 hour per week
  4. D No more than 2 hours per week
Show the answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. No more than 1 hour per week

Why C is right. C. Routine RACES drills and tests share a weekly time limit of 60 minutes in total.

Part 97 lets RACES hold drills and tests to keep its stations ready, up to one hour per week. With approval from the state's chief emergency planning officer, a longer exercise of up to 72 hours may be held no more than twice a calendar year.

“Such drills and tests may not exceed a total time of 1 hour per week.”

47 CFR 97.407(d)(4)

Why not B? The limit is set per week, not per month.
